# Persuasion Elements

Five elements that lift win rate. Each is optional per mandate, but each is a
proven lever. Use the ones the evidence supports, never invent the evidence.

## 1. Business case and ROI

Proposals often describe what will be delivered and skip what it is worth to the
client. Add a short business case that estimates measurable impact.

- Pick the value type that fits: cost saved, time reduced, risk lowered, or revenue
  enabled.
- Put the number in the action title, not only in the body.
- Use before-and-after where you have real figures (for example current handling
  time versus target handling time).
- Show the basis of the estimate in one line, so it is credible, not a slogan.

Rule: every number traces to an input or a clearly stated assumption. If the client
gave no baseline, either ask for one or label the figure an illustrative estimate.
Never present an invented number as a fact.

## 2. Social proof

Turn claims into evidence with real references.

- **Mini case studies**: one or two sentences inside the solution section. Each has
  industry, challenge, solution, and a measurable outcome. For example, "For a
  comparable insurer we delivered a modular claims layer that cut processing time
  by a measured amount."
- **References page**: two or three relevant projects with industry, challenge,
  solution, and outcome, not just client names.
- **Testimonials**: a short cleared quote from a past stakeholder, if available.

Only use references that are real and cleared for use with this client. Do not name
a client reference that has not agreed to be named, and never carry one client's
confidential content into another client's proposal.

## 3. Why us

One concentrated section that answers "why choose you over the competition".

- Three or four differentiators, each a bold claim plus a piece of evidence.
- Position by what makes your approach different, for example deep sector knowledge
  combined with hands-on delivery, rather than breadth.
- Do not name competitors. Ghost a weaker competing approach only if you actually
  know it is in play, by raising the risk it carries, never by inventing a straw
  man.

## 4. Tiered pricing

Give the client a spectrum, not a single yes-or-no number.

- Offer two or three options, for example Essential (core scope), Recommended (full
  scope with optimizations), and Premium (full scope plus ongoing support).
- State clearly what each tier includes and excludes.
- Label the middle option Recommended, so it anchors the decision.
- Keep the commercial conventions the mandate requires (currency, tax note,
  validity date, rate card).

Offering three options tends to raise conversion, because it moves the client from
"do we buy this" to "which option fits us".

## 5. Risks and mitigation

A short risk table shows thoroughness and builds confidence.

- Three to five key risks, each with likelihood, impact, and your mitigation.
- Frame them as challenges you have anticipated, not things that could go wrong.
- Link each mitigation to your method, for example "phased delivery with sprint
  reviews contains scope creep by enabling early course correction".

## The one rule across all five

Never invent facts. A saving, a percentage, a reference client, a certification, or
a rate that is not in an input is an open question, not an assumption. Surface it to
the client or the account lead. A single invented number that the client can
disprove costs more trust than all five elements can build.
