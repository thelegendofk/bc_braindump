# Narrative: SCR and the Pyramid Principle

The backbone of a persuasive proposal is the order of the argument. Two related
tools do the work: the Situation-Complication-Resolution arc for the story, and
the Minto pyramid for every section inside it.

## Why this matters

Research on winning proposals is consistent: a proposal that tells the client's
story, in the client's order, wins more often than one that lists what the vendor
will do. Clients say yes when they feel the proposal is about them.

## Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR)

Structure the proposal, and above all the executive summary, as a three-part arc.

- **Situation**: the client's current state. Facts the client already agrees with.
  This earns trust and shows you listened. Keep it short, it is common ground, not
  news.
- **Complication**: what has changed or what is at risk, and why acting now
  matters. This is the tension that makes the reader want a resolution. Without a
  clear complication, the proposal has no reason to exist yet.
- **Resolution**: your proposal, written as the direct answer to the complication,
  and the outcome it delivers.

SCQA is the same arc with an explicit Question between Complication and Resolution
(Situation, Complication, Question, Answer). Use SCQA when the client's decision
question is worth stating out loud, for example "How can we cut settlement time
without adding regulatory risk?"

### Worked shape for an executive summary

```
Situation:    You operate <context>, and today <current state the client owns>.
Complication: <what changed / what is at risk>, which means <cost or risk> if
              nothing is done, and the window is <why now>.
Question:     How can you <the decision question>?
Resolution:   We propose <the approach in one sentence>, which delivers <outcome
              with a number where possible> within <timeframe>.
```

Test: read only Situation and Complication. Does a reasonable reader now want the
Resolution? If not, sharpen the Complication.

## The Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto)

Inside every section, and on every slide, lead with the conclusion, then give the
support. This is the opposite of building context up to a conclusion at the end.

- **Top**: the single main message of the section, stated first.
- **Middle**: the few supporting arguments (aim for three).
- **Bottom**: the evidence for each argument (data, examples, references).

Two rules keep a pyramid clean:

- Ideas at each level summarize the ideas below them.
- Ideas in a group are the same kind of thing and are in a logical order.

### Before and after

Descriptive (context first, weak):

> We reviewed the current landscape. There are three legacy systems. Integration is
> manual. Data quality varies. Therefore we recommend a phased consolidation.

Pyramid (conclusion first, strong):

> A phased consolidation is the right move, for three reasons. It removes the manual
> integration between the three legacy systems, it fixes the data-quality gaps at
> the source, and it lets you deliver the first result in the first phase rather
> than at the end.

## How SCR and the pyramid fit together

SCR orders the whole proposal. The pyramid orders the inside of each section. The
executive summary is an SCR arc whose Resolution is itself the top of the pyramid
for the rest of the document. Every later section then restates its own conclusion
first and supports it.

## Common mistakes

- Burying the recommendation at the end of a section. Move it to the first line.
- A Situation that is really a sales pitch. It should be neutral common ground.
- No Complication, so there is no urgency and the reader feels no need to decide.
- A Resolution that does not answer the Complication. Cut or rewrite it.
